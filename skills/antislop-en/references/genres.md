# Genre Writing Guide

Shared principles live in the parent `SKILL.md`. This document adds only the
judgments that change by genre. No section requires imitating one person's
fixed style.

## News articles

- Put the most important new fact and its consequence at the top.
- Separate fact, statement and interpretation at the sentence level, and attribute each to its source.
- Do not let a headline assert more strongly than the body. Do not write an unconfirmed causal link as if it were established.
- Add only as much background as the current story needs to be understood.

## Columns and essays

- Do not hide whose viewpoint and judgment this is.
- Use an example as evidence that moves the argument, not decoration around a claim.
- When moving from personal experience to a general conclusion, state the limit of that move.
- End with a change in judgment or an open question, not a slogan repeating what was already said.

## Print pieces

- Reflect the page's hierarchy — headline, subhead, body, sidebar — in the sentences themselves.
- Manage abbreviations and pronoun references so the piece reads without flipping back and forth.
- In a narrow column, check that a long number, address or English-language block does not break the reading flow.
- Because this is read with the eye rather than heard, keep paragraph breaks and punctuation unambiguous.

### Newspaper columns

- Narrow the topic to one sentence and do not hide the writer's judgment. Do not lay out several issues like a department store display.
- Open from a scene, a person, a figure, a statement or a specific document. Do not set the mood first with an era diagnosis or a grand question.
- An example must be evidence for the thesis. Avoid attaching an interesting anecdote and never connecting it back to the argument.
- Separate fact, interpretation and the writer's value judgment at the sentence level. Do not build a straw-man counter-argument; state the strongest version of it, then answer it.
- Within the column's limited space, each paragraph must advance the thesis by one step. If the same judgment repeats in different words, merge or cut paragraphs.
- Do not close with a moral, a lecture to the reader, or vague hope. End with a judgment drawn from the evidence above, a remaining contradiction, a concrete choice, or a scene worth returning to.

## Speeches

- Recap the key terms and logical steps so an audience can follow on a single hearing.
- Adjust sentence length for the speaker's breath and points of emphasis.
- Make address, responsible party, promise and request explicit.
- Use repetition and parallelism only when they build memory or an emotional arc.

### Official statements and addresses

- Distinguish an inaugural address, a commemorative speech, a policy address, a crisis statement and an apology. Do not fill every text with grand rhetoric and patriotic symbols just because the register is official.
- Name the audience precisely at the opening and state the occasion and subject of the remarks. Include ceremonial thanks and historical rhetoric only when the audience needs them to understand the situation.
- Do not blur together the confirmed situation, the government's judgment, the state's and the speaker's responsibility, decisions already made, actions to come, and what is being asked of the public.
- In a statement addressing wrongdoing or harm, state the harm and the responsibility in active sentences. Do not substitute an apology with language that erases the actor and the harm — "it is regrettable that," "there was confusion."
- Attach an actor, a target, a means and a verifiable date to every policy commitment. Do not assert an outcome beyond the speaker's authority or an effect that has not been measured.
- When invoking unity, history or sacrifice, tie it to the actual matter at hand. Do not substitute feeling with unsupported repetition of "a great people," "a new leap forward," "our united strength."
- Make sure one breath, read aloud, carries one central judgment. Use repeated address, parallelism and refrain only where they mark a logical division or emphasize responsibility.
- Close with what the government will do first and the standard by which the public can check it, rather than a vague call to join in.

## Fiction and narrative

- Before naming an emotion in exposition, check whether it can be shown through action, sensation, choice and scene instead.
- Respect point of view and the boundary of what each character can know.
- Do not let every character share the same range of observation and vocabulary.
- Distinguish unfamiliar phrasing from a broken sentence. Keep a deliberate departure from convention when it earns its effect.

## Scripts and dialogue

- Don't pad dialogue to sound like speech; instead, let each character's purpose and relationship show through what they say.
- Don't have characters explain to the audience facts they already know each other, in a way that isn't natural for them.
- Keep stage and action directions to what filming, performance or listening actually needs.
- For subtitled or voiced material, check breath-length per line, homophones, and overlap between what is shown on screen and what is said.

### News and current-affairs reports

- Lead with the newest fact, attributed and timestamped. Divide roles between anchor introduction, reporter package and interview so the same information isn't repeated three times.
- Don't merely describe in words what the footage already shows. Use narration to add cause, scale, sequence and meaning that the footage doesn't carry.
- Give only as many numbers as can be compared in one breath, and state the basis first. Trim a quotation without losing the speaker's standing and context.
- Keep reported fact separate from the reporter's interpretation, and don't convert allegation, estimate or unconfirmed status into a flat declarative.

### Documentary narration

- Let the scene speak first; use narration to bridge time and connect context the footage can't show on its own.
- Don't prescribe emotion in advance with words like "heartbreaking" or "astonishing." Let a subject's action, voice, setting and choices generate the feeling.
- Don't read written material aloud unchanged; rewrite it in word order the ear can follow. Unwind repeated sounds, long modifying clauses and parenthetical asides.

### Short-form and online video

- Open with concrete information or conflict the viewer will get, not manufactured urgency or an inflated question.
- Watch pacing of delivery and cut timing together, not just the runtime in seconds. Give each shot one function for the words spoken over it.
- Don't substitute pressure phrases like "shocking," "chilling," "you have to," "did you not know this yet?" for the actual strength of the fact.
- Don't transcribe the audio in the captions; compress them to what needs to be read — names, figures, points of contrast.

### Interviews, talk and drama dialogue

- Don't load a question with its own answer, or ask about two issues at once. Build a follow-up question from a specific word in the previous answer.
- Don't let every speaker talk like a finished essay. Vary vocabulary, omission, hesitation and sentence length by what each person knows, their relationship, purpose and emotion.
- Don't scatter filler and slang just to imitate speech. Every line should be doing something to the other person.

## Public and legal documents

- State rights, obligations, prohibitions, exceptions, deadlines and the responsible party precisely.
- When simplifying language, do not widen or narrow the legal concept or its scope of application.
- Do not swap a defined term for a synonym; use the same word throughout.
- Let the reader find what they must do in the order of condition, procedure, deadline, then contact.

## Technical documents

- State the outcome the user will get and the prerequisites first.
- Preserve commands, paths, versions, code and option names exactly as given.
- Give one step one verifiable action, with the expected result and what to do on failure.
- Keep settled terms like "API," "JSON" or a product name, but gloss what a first-time reader needs.

## Reports, minutes and operational records

- Distinguish observed fact, interpretation, decision and open item.
- When a conclusion and its evidence sit apart, add a heading or reference so each can be found from the other.
- Do not drop the owner, deadline, dependency or approval status.
- In minutes, preserve the decision and the responsibility accurately, not the speaker's exact phrasing.

### Reports to a chief editor

- Make the report's purpose, key conclusion and the matter needing a decision findable on the first screen or page. Don't bury the conclusion under a long run-up of background.
- Mark fact, reporting result, interpretation, proposal and unconfirmed item with distinct labels in sentences and tables. Don't write an unsourced "industry sources say" or "sources indicate."
- Attach immediately checkable evidence to every conclusion, or point to the section, table or source that carries it. When the evidence supports only part of a conclusion, narrow the claim and state the limit.
- Attach a reference date, population, unit, point of comparison and source to every figure. Don't rename the same concept with different terms, and don't use the same term for two different things.
- Don't lay out material and leave the judgment to the reader. State what the material means, what the options are, what you recommend, and the cost and risk of that recommendation.
- Run a deletion test on every paragraph. If removing it would not change the conclusion, evidence, condition or action, delete it or fold it into a paragraph that needs it.
- Let the heading show the report's actual status. Don't dress up a draft under review, a tentative finding or a matter still being investigated with the same definite-sounding heading as a settled decision.

## Card news and presentation slides

- Give each screen one central claim or fact, and don't let the title and body repeat the same words.
- Even when shortened, don't change the meaning by dropping the actor, condition, basis of comparison or source.
- Let a single card be understood on its own, while keeping the flow and conclusion connected across cards.
- In a presenter's script, don't just read the on-screen text aloud; spell out the evidence, examples and reason for each transition in speech.

## Email and business prose

- Make the purpose and what the recipient must do findable in the first paragraph.
- Separate necessary background, the request or answer, and the deadline and responsible party.
- Don't let politeness blur the key judgment, and don't stack unnecessary hedges.
- Let the subject line state the email's business and status precisely, and make clear whether a reply is needed.

## Blog and social posts

- Let the title and first paragraph accurately preview the actual content.
- Don't reach for a claim stronger than the evidence, manufactured urgency or an inflated question just to catch attention.
- Work search terms naturally into context rather than repeating the same phrase mechanically.
- Include a call to action only when the piece's purpose calls for one, and keep it to one clear ask.
